LESSON 20 / 27 · ORIGINAL TEACHING EXAMPLES

Run it later: a durable job scheduler

Worked interview • leases and duplicate effects • 45 minutes

Prompt and clarifications

Exact prompt: “Design a durable scheduler for Corner Market. Merchants schedule sale reminders and nightly inventory reports. Jobs must survive crashes, run close to their requested time, retry safely, and support cancellation.” Durable means acknowledged work remains recoverable after the failures covered by the storage contract. Scheduling decides when work becomes eligible; workers perform the actual effects.

  • One-time or recurring? Assume both. Recurrence uses an explicit named timezone and a defined daylight-saving policy; store each generated occurrence separately.
  • Timing precision? Assume 99% of eligible jobs start within 30 seconds when dependencies are healthy. This is a start-delay target, not a completion promise.
  • Delivery semantics? Assume jobs may execute more than once. The observable business effect should be deduplicated where possible.
  • Cancellation? Define “start” as the successful atomic claim transition into RUNNING. Cancellation that wins before that claim prevents execution; after the claim it is best effort, even if the worker has not yet called the external provider. An email already accepted by an external provider may be impossible to recall.

Numbers that drive choices

Assume 1,000,000 jobs/day, an average runtime of 2 seconds, and a peak arrival rate ten times average. Average rate is 1,000,000 / 86,400 = 11.57 jobs/second; round peak to 116 jobs/second. Little's Law relates average work in progress to throughput times duration in a stable system: at peak, 116 × 2 = 232 concurrent executions. At a planning utilization of 70%, need about 232 / 0.7 = 332 execution slots. Slots are concurrency capacity, not necessarily machines; CPU-heavy and waiting-heavy jobs require different sizing.

At 1 KB of metadata per job and 30 days of retained history, 30 million rows require about 30 GB of raw metadata, before indexes and replication. Large report payloads belong in object storage with references in the job record. If all one million jobs arrive at midnight, the tenfold peak assumption is false: identify scheduled bursts, introduce allowed jitter, and provision or negotiate the completion window.

Other goals: no acknowledged job silently disappears after a single worker failure, tenant isolation, bounded retries, auditable state changes, and a clearly documented disaster recovery point. Recovery point objective is the acceptable amount of recent data loss; recovery time objective is the acceptable restoration duration. Neither follows automatically from “database replication.”

Idempotency means repeating the same logical request has the same intended effect. Reuse of a key with a different request body returns a conflict instead of silently changing the job. Authenticate ownership before reads and cancellations. Permit only registered job types and validated parameters; arbitrary uploaded code is a different, much harder isolation problem.

Progressive design: transaction first

Start with a database-backed queue. Workers select due pending rows inside a short transaction, acquire row locks, and update them to running with a lease. A lease grants temporary ownership until a timestamp. With PostgreSQL, FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED lets competing workers skip already locked rows; its inconsistent view is suitable for queue-like selection, not general reporting. Commit the claim before doing slow external work. Holding database locks while generating a report or calling an email service ties storage availability to slow dependencies.

Each claim increments lease_generation. Completion is conditional on matching job ID, running state, and generation. A stale worker whose lease expired cannot overwrite a newer worker's result. This generation is a fencing token, a monotonically increasing ownership marker. It fences database state; an external effect also needs its own deduplication or fencing support. Heartbeats extend leases with the same generation. Pick a lease longer than normal execution and renew well before expiry, while accepting that pauses and network partitions can still cause overlapping workers.

Poll due work every second with a bounded batch. Use the database clock consistently for due and lease comparisons. Add randomized poll delays and an index so idle workers do not repeatedly scan history. Separate short notifications from long reports, and enforce tenant concurrency limits so one merchant cannot occupy every slot. A broker can later transport ready jobs, but the database remains the authority for scheduled eligibility; publishing must then use a transactional outbox to avoid losing jobs between commit and send.

The unavoidable duplicate window

Consider a worker that sends a reminder successfully and crashes before marking the job completed. The lease expires and another worker retries. Marking complete before sending instead creates a lost-effect window. A scheduler alone cannot make an arbitrary external side effect happen exactly once. Pass the stable occurrence ID as the downstream idempotency key when the provider supports a documented durable deduplication contract. Otherwise record the uncertainty and choose explicitly between possible duplicates and possible omission.

For reports, write output under a stable job-derived object key and publish a manifest conditionally. For internal database work, insert the business effect and mark completion in the same transaction when both share a database. A unique constraint on effect ID is the final concurrency defense. Retain deduplication records long enough to cover retries, replays, and backup restoration; a five-minute record is insufficient if operators can replay a month later.

Retries, cancellation, and failure

Classify permanent validation errors separately from temporary timeouts. Retry temporary failures with exponential backoff and random jitter, cap both attempts and elapsed time, and stop after the job deadline. A dead-letter state preserves exhausted work for inspection; it is not an unmonitored trash folder. Alert on oldest eligible age, failure rate by class, lease expiry, and queue depth. Compare incoming work with sustainable completion rate: if arrival exceeds processing, no retry policy can prevent backlog growth.

Cancellation uses a conditional update. Pending becomes cancelled; running becomes cancel_requested, and the worker checks between safe steps. Cancellation and completion racing must have an explicitly allowed outcome. Recurring schedules need a stable occurrence key such as schedule ID plus intended UTC instant, a timezone database version or documented update policy, and a choice for missed runs after outages: catch up all, latest only, or skip. Never “just add 24 hours” to model every local calendar day.

Database failover can repeat claims if recent writes were lost. Ensure the promised recovery point matches business expectations, keep effects idempotent, and test restoration. A worker that loses connectivity must stop starting new effects once it cannot renew ownership; a paused process resuming later still requires fencing. Large outages may create retry storms, so recover gradually with global and tenant admission controls.

Alternatives, evolution, and assessment

Reject an in-memory timer on an API server: restarts erase it, replicas disagree, and deployments shift responsibility. Reject a single cron entry per job at this scale: mutation, cancellation, observability, and ownership become fragile. Begin with indexed due rows; shard by tenant or due-time bucket only when measured lock contention or storage demands it. Partition old history for retention and benchmark due-row queries at realistic backlog sizes. Cost follows retained metadata, polling frequency, execution duration, and repeated work.

Practice: the job takes 90 seconds but its lease is 30 seconds. Diagnose and redesign.

Without renewal, another worker can start at 30 seconds. Renew the lease conditionally, measure heartbeat health, and retain generation checks. This reduces overlaps but does not eliminate them; make effects idempotent. For a non-idempotent external operation, reconcile its status after an uncertain response rather than blindly retrying.

Interviewer follow-ups and rubric

Follow-ups: What happens after a week-long outage? How do you stop a noisy tenant? Can an operator replay a succeeded job? Strong: separates start from completion, calculates concurrency, explains the duplicate window, and shows conditional ownership transitions. Weak: promises exactly-once email, holds locks through network calls, or treats a queue as a complete recurring scheduler.

A short spoken walkthrough

Candidate: Does no duplicate job mean one accepted owner or one external side effect?
Interviewer: The business wants one reminder email.
Candidate: I will start with the simplest durable path and state the boundary before distributing it.
Interviewer: A paused worker wakes after its lease expires. Are we safe?
Candidate: The new epoch fences state writes only where the receiver checks it. For email I need provider idempotency on a stable effect ID or must disclose the duplicate-versus-loss tradeoff after an unknown result. I cannot promise one email merely because the queue has one current owner.
