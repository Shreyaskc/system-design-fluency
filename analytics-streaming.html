LESSON 22 / 27 · ORIGINAL TEACHING EXAMPLES

Count what happened: merchant analytics and streaming

Worked interview • time, replay, and accounting boundaries • 45 minutes

Prompt and assumed answers

Exact prompt: “Build Corner Market's merchant dashboard showing product views, checkout conversion, and paid order totals within one minute. Support historical reports and recovery after processing outages.” Streaming means processing a continuing sequence of events incrementally. An event is a record of something that happened, with an identity and occurrence time; it is not automatically a unique or trustworthy fact.

  • Is the dashboard financially authoritative? Assume view and funnel numbers are approximate operational analytics. Paid totals originate from confirmed order transitions and are reconciled against the order database. Settlement reporting is separate.
  • How fresh? Assume 95% of valid events become queryable within 60 seconds during healthy operation. Late events can correct recent windows for 24 hours.
  • How long retained? Assume 7 days in the replay log, 1 year in object storage, and 90 days of fine-grained aggregates.
  • Can clients resend? Yes. Client timestamps may be wrong, and mobile devices can upload later. Trust server-recorded order events more than client claims.

Capacity and contracts

Assume 100,000 daily shoppers, each producing 50 product-interaction events/day. That is 5,000,000 events/day, or 57.87/second average. Tenfold peak is about 579/second. At 500 bytes per encoded event, raw traffic is 2.5 GB/day, 17.5 GB for seven days, or 52.5 GB with three log copies, before protocol overhead and indexes. A year of raw events is approximately 912.5 GB before compression and redundant storage. Compression is workload-dependent; benchmark rather than claiming a universal ratio.

Assume 2,000 merchants, 20 relevant products per merchant, and two event counters per one-minute window. A dense upper bound is 2,000 × 20 × 2 × 1,440 = 115.2 million counter cells/day. Sparse activity, coarser historical windows, and retaining only useful dimensions can save far more than changing storage brands. Do not materialize every user, product, region, campaign, and device combination without a query requirement.

The proposed ingestion target is 99.9% monthly successful server acknowledgments after the configured durable log write. Define which failures that durability covers. Track ingestion latency separately from end-to-end freshness. A service can accept every event while its dashboard is hours stale.

Event contract and architecture

Use an allowlist of event types, maximum batch size, schema validation, and rate limits. A schema version identifies the shape of data; a metric-definition version identifies the meaning of a computed number. They solve different problems. Server-issued merchant identity prevents one merchant writing or reading another merchant's data. Minimize personal fields and define consent, deletion, and retention requirements before collecting them.

Start simple, then make time explicit

Begin with server-side order events written through the transactional outbox and a batch dashboard refreshed every minute. Add client views through validated ingestion. Partition the log by merchant when ordered merchant processing is useful, but recognize that one large merchant can become a hot partition. Partitioning by merchant plus a stable subkey spreads load at the cost of merging partial aggregates. Log order is normally scoped to a partition; do not assume one global ordering of every shopper's actions.

Distinguish event time, when the action occurred, from processing time, when the processor handled it. A phone records a view at 12:00 but reconnects at 12:10. Counting by processing time puts the view in the wrong minute. Count valid events into event-time windows, with a policy for implausible client timestamps. Use received time or quarantine when timestamps are outside accepted bounds, and disclose this interpretation in metric definitions.

A watermark is the processor's estimate of how far event-time progress has advanced. It is a policy informed by observations, not proof that older events can never arrive. With a two-minute lateness allowance, a window can publish a provisional value promptly, then accept updates. Our contract permits corrections for 24 hours; older arrivals go to a repair path or a separately labeled late-event count. Define what a dashboard displays when a previous total changes.

Duplicates, replay, and the denominator

Deduplicate by stable event ID within an explicit retention window. At 5 million events/day and 24 hours of deduplication, 32 bytes per ID is 160 MB raw before state-store overhead. A duplicated event arriving after that window can be counted again unless archival repair or a longer-lived unique record catches it. Do not sell bounded deduplication as eternal uniqueness.

Make aggregate writes replay-safe. One option writes deterministic window values keyed by window and processor version, replacing prior values rather than blindly incrementing on every delivery. Another commits processed offsets and output in a transactional boundary supported by the chosen stack. Broker-level transactional processing does not make arbitrary external databases, emails, or downstream APIs exactly once. Verify the entire boundary. During repair, write a new version of aggregates and atomically switch readers after validation instead of mixing old and new partial results.

Conversion needs a definition. “Paid orders / views” is not the same as “sessions with a paid order / sessions with a product view.” Suppose 1,000 eligible sessions contain views and 80 of those sessions pay: conversion is 80 / 1,000 = 8%. If those sessions generated 4,000 views, dividing 80 by 4,000 yields 2%, a different metric. Establish attribution window, identity rules, bot treatment, and timezone boundaries before implementing joins. Cross-device identity and missing events limit certainty; display suitable caveats.

Failure and operational decisions

If a processor is down for two hours while traffic averages 58/second, backlog is about 417,600 events. A recovering processor able to handle 300/second while 58/second continue arriving drains at 242/second, requiring about 1,726 seconds, or 28.8 minutes. Simply dividing by 300 understates recovery time. Provision and alert using remaining retention headroom: once seven-day log data expire, recovery needs the object archive.

Poison events must not stall an entire partition forever. Preserve failed events with reason and schema version in a quarantined stream, count them, and provide a controlled replay tool. For a query-store outage, stop or buffer within bounded capacity rather than silently advancing progress and dropping output. Track oldest unprocessed event, source-to-dashboard delay, duplicates, late-event rate, invalid events, state growth, and reconciliation differences.

Reject querying the production order database for every dashboard refresh: analytics scans can impair checkout and cannot reconstruct client views. Reject treating a cache increment as an auditable financial total. Reject starting with a complex stream platform if minute-level batch satisfies requirements. Add streaming when latency, volume, and operational ownership justify it; ongoing cost includes replicas, retained data, state checkpoints, repair jobs, and on-call expertise.

Evolution and practice

Keep immutable source events under retention rules, version transformations, and support deterministic backfills. If regulations require deletion, an append-only slogan does not override it: implement deletion or cryptographic and storage controls appropriate to the requirement, and propagate effects to derived data. Keep order correctness outside the analytics path so a dashboard outage cannot stop checkout.

Practice: a replay doubles paid totals. What failed, and how do you repair it?

The sink probably increments totals without deduplicating event IDs or replacing deterministic windows. Pause publication, rebuild a new aggregate version from trusted order events, compare totals to authoritative orders, and switch readers after validation. Preserve the bad run for audit without exposing it as current data.

Follow-ups and rubric

Follow-ups: What does “one minute” measure? How do definitions change? What happens beyond retention? Strong: distinguishes times, derives backlog recovery, defines denominators, and bounds deduplication. Weak: promises exactly-once end to end from a broker setting, ignores late events, or confuses dashboard totals with settlement accounting.

A short spoken walkthrough

Candidate: Are these dashboard counts provisional or financial settlement records?
Interviewer: Provisional product dashboards with later corrections.
Candidate: I will start with the simplest durable path and state the boundary before distributing it.
Interviewer: A replay sends every event again. Will counts double?
Candidate: Not if identity, retention and aggregate replacement are designed together. I deduplicate stable event IDs within the declared horizon and rebuild into a new version before cutover. Events older than that horizon require a separate controlled backfill. Financial settlement would need stronger source reconciliation and an explicit accounting contract.
