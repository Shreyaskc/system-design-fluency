2. Estimate load with units and uncertainty

Listen to this chapter →

Estimate to change a decision

Capacity estimation is a simplified model of resources needed to serve a workload. It is useful when it chooses between designs or reveals an impossible promise. Corner Market has 100,000 daily active shoppers, each opening twenty catalog pages and placing 0.2 orders per day. These are explicit fictional baseline assumptions used throughout this foundation sequence. A day contains 86,400 seconds. Assume a tenfold peak-to-average factor for requests, a 2 KB decimal catalog response excluding photos, a 1 KB order record before indexes, and thirty-day hot order retention.

Catalog traffic is 100,000 × 20 = 2,000,000 requests/day. Dividing by 86,400 gives approximately 23.15 requests/second on average and 231.5 at the assumed peak. Orders are 100,000 × 0.2 = 20,000/day, approximately 0.2315/second average and 2.315/second peak. Do not say “millions of users require sharding”: this baseline may fit a modest database. Search, photos, expensive joins, geographic distance, and bursts may matter more than total user count.

Keep units attached

Two million catalog responses × 2,000 bytes equals 4,000,000,000 bytes/day, or 4 GB using decimal units. Peak response bandwidth is 231.5 × 2,000 = 463,000 bytes/second, approximately 3.704 megabits/second before headers, encryption, retries, and images. Eight bits make one byte. A gigabyte is 10^9 bytes; a gibibyte is 2^30 bytes. State which convention you use, especially when comparing storage bills and memory capacity.

Orders consume 20,000 × 1,000 = 20 MB/day of raw rows, or 600 MB in thirty days. If an illustrative combined allowance for row overhead and indexes doubles raw storage, the working primary needs 1.2 GB. Three full database copies require 3.6 GB for those rows and indexes, excluding write logs, backups, free space, and unrelated tables. Replication is a copy multiplier, not a substitute for estimating backup history. A deletion policy changes both cost and recovery requirements.

Concurrency and service time

Concurrency is work in progress. For a stable system, average work in progress equals arrival rate times average time in the system; this is Little's Law. At 231.5 requests/second and 0.2 seconds average response time, approximately 46.3 requests are in flight. This does not mean forty-seven CPU cores are required: much of the time may be waiting for a database or network. If each request actually consumes 5 milliseconds of CPU time, that workload consumes 1.1575 CPU-seconds per second before overhead, suggesting more than one fully utilized core and headroom for bursts.

Utilization is the fraction of resource capacity in use. Queueing delays rise sharply as arrivals approach service capacity, so a benchmark at maximum throughput is not a safe operating target. Measure throughput together with tail latency: p99 is the response time below which ninety-nine percent of observations fall. Percentiles do not add across services. A whole-request p99 cannot be obtained by simply adding each dependency's p99 because their slow events may overlap or occur on different requests.

Model the awkward workload

A flash sale can invalidate the tenfold assumption. Suppose 5,000 shoppers attempt to reserve the same item within one second. Total catalog load remains unremarkable, but one inventory row receives 5,000 contending writes. A hot key is one identifier receiving disproportionate traffic. More database shards do not divide one indivisible stock counter. Consider admission control, a waiting room, partitioned stock allocations with reconciliation, or accepting fewer requests while preserving the invariant. Select the mechanism only after discussing acceptable customer behavior.

Use sensitivity analysis: recompute the result when an uncertain input changes. At 200 KB per page instead of 2 KB, catalog transfer becomes 400 GB/day. At a hundredfold peak rather than tenfold, peak request rate becomes 2,315/second. Write low, expected, and high assumptions; identify which measurement you would collect first. Load tests should reproduce skew, cold caches, slow dependencies, and retry storms, not just uniform random successful reads.

Practice: a worker drains 150 jobs/second while 250 arrive each second for two minutes. What backlog forms?

The deficit is 100 jobs/second, so 12,000 jobs accumulate after 120 seconds, assuming zero initial backlog and constant rates. When arrivals fall to 50/second, net drainage is 100/second, requiring another 120 seconds. “Autoscale workers” is incomplete unless the downstream database can absorb their added load.

Exit artifact: a worksheet with assumptions, equations, units, peak factors, uncertainty ranges, and the architectural decision each estimate informs. Recalculate without a calculator first, then verify arithmetic; order-of-magnitude mistakes should become visible before precision distracts you.

Request trace: Resource bounds

At a flash sale, 5,000 shoppers arrive; a bounded admission queue accepts a safe subset; the rest receive explicit retry guidance. If each rejected client retries immediately, offered traffic grows. Keep admitted work below sustainable service capacity and test the retry policy, rather than multiplying server counts from daily averages.
