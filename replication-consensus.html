6. Replicate data without losing the meaning of success

Copies solve different problems

Replication maintains multiple copies of data. It can improve read capacity and help survive a machine failure, but copies can disagree. Corner Market starts with a leader database accepting writes and replicas following its change log. A leader is the node authorized to sequence changes for a dataset. Replication lag is the delay between a leader's change and a replica applying it. A fresh reservation may therefore exist on the leader while a replica still reports no reservation.

Asynchronous replication acknowledges a write before a remote copy confirms it; synchronous replication waits for specified confirmation. Neither label is enough: does confirmation mean received in memory, durably written, or applied and visible? Waiting for more distant copies generally increases latency and makes success depend on more network paths. If only an asynchronous replica survives a leader crash, recently acknowledged writes may be absent. State this as a potential recovery-point loss rather than promising durability from the existence of replicas.

Specify a consistency guarantee

Consistency describes which observations a distributed system permits. Linearizability makes each operation appear to take effect at one instant between its invocation and response, respecting real-time order. Eventual consistency says replicas converge after updates stop and communication recovers; it gives no specific time bound without additional assumptions. Read-your-writes means a client observes its own acknowledged updates. These guarantees are not interchangeable and should be selected for operations, not casually attached to an entire product.

For a shopper immediately opening a new reservation, route that session's read to the leader, or carry a replication position and wait for a replica to catch up before serving it. The latter needs a deadline and fallback. Catalog reads may tolerate lag, while inventory decrements require one authoritative ordering. A consistent database does not make an independent cache consistent. Follow the complete response path when stating the guarantee.

Consensus and fencing

Consensus is agreement among nodes on a value or ordered sequence despite some failures. Raft uses leaders, terms, and replicated logs; a majority is central to its commit and election rules. In a five-node voting group, a majority is three, allowing progress with two unavailable nodes when the remaining nodes can communicate under the protocol's assumptions. The original Raft paper, checked 2026-10-19, explains safety and leader election. This does not make a five-copy application automatically a correct consensus implementation.

During a network split, a former leader may continue running even after a new leader is elected elsewhere. Preventing two writers requires fencing: downstream storage rejects operations from obsolete leadership generations. A monotonically increasing fencing token is useful only if the resource enforces it atomically. A lock lease alone is unsafe when a process pauses beyond its lease, resumes, and writes as though it still owns the lock. Timeouts detect suspicion, not certainty of death.

Availability during partitions

A partition is a communication failure that prevents some nodes from exchanging messages. For operations requiring a single agreed stock count, a disconnected minority should reject or defer writes rather than independently selling the same last item. That sacrifices immediate write availability to preserve the chosen correctness promise. Browsing cached descriptions can continue with an explicit stale-data policy. “The service is available” is too broad when different operations intentionally behave differently.

Quorum arithmetic needs assumptions. With three replicas, write acknowledgments from two and reads from two have intersecting sets, but intersection alone does not ensure linearizable reads. Version selection, concurrent writes, failed partial writes, leadership, and repair behavior matter. Do not derive a guarantee solely from R + W > N. Explain the actual algorithm or rely on a documented database guarantee that matches the operation.

Recovery is an operational procedure

A replica faithfully reproduces accidental deletes, so replication is not a backup. Backups preserve recoverable historical states; test restoration, ownership, access, and replay to a target point. Failover includes detection, election or promotion, fencing, routing updates, client reconnects, and data reconciliation. Test the entire sequence. Observe lag by time and log position, last successful backup restoration, rejected stale writes, and application-visible errors during failover.

Practice: can an isolated store keep selling its locally cached stock?

Only if the business accepts possible overselling, or stock was previously allocated into a bounded store-owned quota that cannot overlap other sellers. A quota changes the model: each side may spend only its allocation. On reconnection, reconcile allocations and sales. Simply copying the shared stock count to both sides does not create independent ownership.

Exit artifact: state one read guarantee and one write guarantee, draw a partition, and identify which requests succeed, wait, or fail. Include the acknowledged data that could be lost under your selected replication policy.

Request trace: One accepted writer

A reserve request reaches the elected leader; its update is replicated according to the selected durability contract; only then is success returned. If the leader disappears before acknowledgment, the outcome may remain uncertain. Reuse request identity after failover and fence stale leaders so retries cannot create a second hold.
